Abstract

Land-cover mapping is a foundational pillar of earth observation, environmental monitoring, agricultural yield assessment, and natural hazard management. In heterogeneous natural and anthropogenic terrains, satellite image footprints frequently encompass multiple surface categories simultaneously (e.g., agricultural parcels bordered by woodlots, or stream corridors traversing settlements). Conventional single-label classification assigns an exclusive category to each scene, fundamentally misrepresenting mixed landscapes. In this project, we implement and evaluate a multi-label remote sensing classification framework using Sentinel-2 multispectral imagery from the benchmark BigEarthNet v2.0 (reBEN) archive.

We curated and verified a 500-patch genuine dataset originating from Sentinel-2 tile T33UUP in the Austrian Central Alps, partitioned into official, disjoint splits: 300 Training, 100 Validation, and 100 Test patches. Six diagnostic spectral bands—Blue (B02), Green (B03), Red (B04), Near-Infrared (B08), and Shortwave-Infrared (B11, B12)—were radiometrically converted to Bottom-Of-Atmosphere (BOA) reflectance [0.0, 1.0], with 20 m SWIR bands harmonized to 10 m via bilinear interpolation.

We trained a regularized 4-block CNN (MultispectralCNN, ~617k parameters) under BCEWithLogitsLoss and conducted a controlled band ablation comparing visible RGB (M1), visible plus NIR (M2), and full multispectral inputs (M3). At standard threshold T=0.5, M2 achieved the highest overall performance (Macro-F1 0.7110, Micro-F1 0.8205, Exact Match 38.0%), outperforming baseline M1 on the observed test metrics (Macro-F1 0.5978, Micro-F1 0.7403, Exact Match 12.0%). NIR provided a decisive +11.32 percentage point gain, resolving woody scrub confusion (Shrub F1 jumped from 0.3529 to 0.9114) and water delineation (F1 0.8947). Conversely, M3 underperformed M2 (Macro-F1 0.5673, Exact Match 5.0%), with small-sample dimensionality, resampling, and optimization instability as possible contributing factors to grassland commission errors (57 false positives). Furthermore, all models recorded 0.0000 F1 on Wetland at T=0.5 and validation-tuned T*=0.5 due to extreme training imbalance (only 8 positive training patches).

1. Introduction

1.1 The Imperative for Satellite Land-Cover Mapping

Automated, high-fidelity land-use and land-cover (LULC) identification from spaceborne sensors is essential for tracking urbanization, deforestation, crop phenology, and water resources. Modern multispectral instruments, notably ESA's Sentinel-2 constellation, offer high radiometric resolution, 5-day revisit cycles, and broad coverage across visible, near-infrared (NIR), and shortwave-infrared (SWIR) wavelengths.

1.2 Limitations of Single-Label Classification

In standard remote sensing classification, each observation unit is forced into an exclusive class: y ∈ {1, 2, ..., K}. However, at typical patch dimensions (1.2 km × 1.2 km, 120 × 120 pixels at 10 m resolution), genuine earth surfaces are inherently heterogeneous. Within a single patch, one frequently observes cultivated fields adjacent to farmhouse clusters, a stream flowing past pastures, or dense woodland fringing scrubland. Single-label classification arbitrarily suppresses subordinate classes or fails on mixed pixels.

1.3 Multi-Label Formulation

To model landscape complexity without artificial mutual exclusivity, the ground truth for each patch is structured as a binary multi-hot vector:

y = [y1, y2, ..., yK]T ∈ {0, 1}K

where yk = 1 denotes the presence of land-cover category k, and yk = 0 denotes its absence. Under this formulation, multiple labels can legitimately evaluate to 1 simultaneously. In our curated dataset, 94.4% of patches contain ≥ 2 co-occurring classes (mean: 3.00 classes per patch; test mean: 3.07).

1.4 Central Research Questions

  1. Can visible, NIR, and SWIR Sentinel-2 observations accurately identify multiple co-occurring surface types in complex landscapes?
  2. Does adding NIR (B08) and SWIR (B11, B12) consistently improve classification accuracy over standard visible RGB, and how does spectral dimensionality interact with small training sample sizes?

2. Dataset and Spectral Bands

2.1 The BigEarthNet v2.0 (reBEN) Dataset

We employ the open-access BigEarthNet v2.0 benchmark archive (Clasen et al., 2024; Sumbul et al., 2019, 2021; bigearth.net), released by the Remote Sensing Image Analysis (RSiM) and DIMA groups at Technische Universität Berlin. BigEarthNet v2.0 represents a major scientific upgrade over legacy v1.0:

  • Atmospheric Correction: All Sentinel-2 tiles are processed to Level-2A Bottom-Of-Atmosphere (BOA) surface reflectance via sen2cor v2.11.
  • Label Integrity: Ground truth labels were re-extracted using the CORINE Land Cover 2018 database (CLC2018 v2020_u1).
  • Official Benchmark Splits: Partitions preserve official BigEarthNet v2.0 benchmark split assignments.

2.2 Geographic Provenance & Dataset Partitions

To enable rigorous, fully reproducible model training and Colab compatibility without downloading the prohibitive 59 GiB archive, we curated a genuine 500-patch dataset from the first tile in BigEarthNet: Tile T33UUP, situated in the Austrian Central Alps (UTM Zone 33N).

  • Landscape Context: Alpine and sub-alpine topography (coniferous and mixed forests, managed pastures, agricultural valleys, settlements, mountain streams).
  • Official Split Assignments: 300 Train (60.0%), 100 Validation (20.0%), 100 Test (20.0%).
  • Patch-Level Disjointness: Strictly disjoint partitions (Train ∩ Val = 0, Train ∩ Test = 0, Val ∩ Test = 0).
  • Inspection Patch Protocol: All 5 Phase 1 inspection patches (T33UUP_26_57, 27_55, 27_56, 27_57, 27_58) reside strictly in the Test split.
  • Distinction from Geographic Independence: Because all 500 patches originate from Tile T33UUP, patches share regional terrain, atmospheric overpass, and phenology. The protocol guarantees patch-level disjointness but does not constitute geographic independence across regions.

2.5 Preprocessing and Harmonization Pipeline

Raw Sentinel-2 Level-2A GeoTIFFs provide 16-bit integer Digital Numbers (DN). Preprocessing transforms raw files into harmonized physical tensors:

  1. Bilinear Spatial Resampling: Bands B11 and B12 have a native resolution of 20 m (60 × 60 pixels). We resample them to the 10 m working grid (120 × 120 pixels) using bilinear interpolation (PIL.Image.BILINEAR, order=1). Bilinear interpolation computes distance-weighted averages of four adjacent pixel centers, generating smooth continuous spatial gradients without ringing artifacts (unlike bicubic) or artificial blocky steps (unlike nearest neighbor).
  2. Radiometric Scaling to Surface Reflectance: In accordance with the ESA Sentinel-2 L2A product specification, surface reflectance ρ is quantified as DN = 10,000 × ρ. We apply:
    ρ = clip(DN / 10,000.0, 0.0, 1.0)
    Division by 10,000 converts raw DN to Bottom-Of-Atmosphere surface reflectance. Clipping to [0.0, 1.0] handles rare specular glint highlights (e.g., solar reflections on glass roofs, greenhouse panels, or calm water surfaces > 10,000 DN, representing < 0.0042% of all pixels).
  3. Preservation of Raw Data: All raw GeoTIFF files remain completely unmodified in data/curated_patches/. Preprocessed tensors are saved in compressed archive data/curated_dataset_phase2_1.npz (76.66 MB).

3.3 Multi-Label Co-Occurrence Patterns & Spectral Signatures

In our Austrian alpine dataset, co-occurring pairs highlight common ecological mosaics:

  • Forest + Grassland/Pasture: The most frequent pairing, characteristic of alpine pastureland bordered by montane spruce and mixed woods.
  • Agriculture + Grassland: Dominant in valley bottoms where cultivated forage parcels and permanent pastures form fine-grained mosaics.
  • Agriculture + Built-up: Ubiquitous across rural settlements, villages, and farm outbuildings.

Spectral profiles across the 6 bands demonstrate distinct physical characteristics:

  • Forest: Strong chlorophyll absorption in Red (B04 ~0.02), a steep red-edge jump to NIR (B08 ~0.38), and moderate SWIR reflectance (B11 ~0.18, B12 ~0.09).
  • Water: Low overall reflectance, with sharp absorption falling to near-zero in NIR and SWIR.
  • Built-up: Monotonically rising reflectance across visible into SWIR without the characteristic red-edge peak.
  • Agriculture vs. Grassland: Both display vegetative red-edge curves; actively fertilized arable crops display higher NIR peaks, while pastures display higher visible soil background when grazed.
  • Wetland & Shrub: Wetlands combine foliar greenness with high SWIR water absorption; shrubs show intermediate NIR reflectance, lower than mature dense forest canopy due to open structure.

4. Methodology: Multi-Label Deep Learning

4.1 Multi-Label Problem Formulation

Let X ∈ ℝC × 120 × 120 represent an input patch. The neural network computes unconstrained raw logits z = [z1, ..., zK]T ∈ ℝ7. Unlike multi-class softmax classification, multi-label learning models each class as an independent Bernoulli trial. Independent class probabilities are produced via the standard logistic sigmoid function:

p̂k = σ(zk) = 1 / (1 + e-zk) ∈ [0, 1]

4.2 Network Architecture: MultispectralCNN

We designed MultispectralCNN in src/model.py to extract spatial-spectral features without excessive parameterization:

  • Input: Accepts C ∈ {3, 4, 6} channels at 120 × 120 resolution.
  • Block 1 (120×120 → 60×60): Conv2d(C→32, 3×3) + BN + ReLU + Conv2d(32→32, 3×3) + BN + ReLU + MaxPool2d(2, 2)
  • Block 2 (60×60 → 30×30): Conv2d(32→64, 3×3) + BN + ReLU + Conv2d(64→64, 3×3) + BN + ReLU + MaxPool2d(2, 2)
  • Block 3 (30×30 → 15×15): Conv2d(64→128, 3×3) + BN + ReLU + Conv2d(128→128, 3×3) + BN + ReLU + MaxPool2d(2, 2)
  • Block 4 (15×15 → 7×7): Conv2d(128→256, 3×3) + BN + ReLU + MaxPool2d(2, 2)
  • Global Pooling & Head: AdaptiveAvgPool2d((1, 1)) → Flatten → Dropout(0.4) → Linear(256→128) → ReLU → Dropout(0.3) → Linear(128→7).
  • Trainable Parameters: M1: 616,679; M2: 616,967; M3: 617,543.

4.3 Training Protocols and Optimization

Models were trained on the 300-patch training split using reproducible settings:

  • Loss Function: Binary Cross-Entropy with Logits (BCEWithLogitsLoss).
  • Optimizer: AdamW (learning rate = 1.0 × 10-3, weight decay = 1.0 × 10-4).
  • LR Schedule: CosineAnnealingLR (Tmax = 35, ηmin = 1.0 × 10-5).
  • Batch Size: 16 (yielding 19 parameter updates per epoch).
  • Early Stopping: Monitored on validation loss with patience of 8 epochs (restored best checkpoint).
  • Reproducibility: Random seed 42 fixed for Python, NumPy, and PyTorch.
  • Zero Test Leakage: Model weights, normalization statistics, and decision thresholds never touched test labels.

4.4 Multi-Label Loss and Decision Threshold Calibration

Binary cross-entropy computes the average loss across all classes:

LBCE(z, y) = - (1/K) ∑k=1K [ yk log σ(zk) + (1 - yk) log(1 - σ(zk)) ]

Binary predictions ŷk ∈ {0, 1} are formed by applying a threshold vector T = [T1, ..., T7]:

ŷk = 𝕀(p̂k ≥ Tk)

We evaluate two thresholding protocols:

  1. Standard Default Threshold (Tk = 0.50): Canonical unbiased cutoff for logistic sigmoid.
  2. Validation-Calibrated Thresholds (Tk*): Selected by grid search over T ∈ [0.20, 0.80] strictly on validation probabilities to maximize validation Macro-F1. Test labels were never accessed during threshold selection.

4.5 Band Contribution Experiments

To isolate spectral utility, three controlled models were trained under identical seeds, splits, and architectures:

  • M1 (Visible RGB): Channels [B02, B03, B04] (3 inputs; 490, 560, 665 nm)
  • M2 (Visible + NIR): Channels [B02, B03, B04, B08] (4 inputs; + 842 nm)
  • M3 (Full Multispectral): Channels [B02, B03, B04, B08, B11, B12] (6 inputs; + 1610, 2190 nm)

6. Error and Co-Occurrence Analysis

6.1 Diagnostic Breakdown of Test Predictions

Test set predictions at standard threshold T=0.50 demonstrate:

  • Exact Match Ratio: 38.0% in M2, 12.0% in M1, and 5.0% in M3.
  • Grassland Commission Errors in M3: M3 predicted Grassland in 95 out of 100 test patches, generating 57 false alarms and collapsing precision to 40.0%.
  • Water Omission Errors in M3: M3 missed 15 out of 21 water patches (recall 28.57%), down from 17 true positives in M2.

6.2 Analysis of Confusing Class Combinations

Case 1: Agriculture ↔ Grassland / Pasture

Ecological Overlap: Cultivated forage crops, fallow arable parcels, and permanent pastures share similar green leaf pigments and visual texture. In visible wavelengths, both exhibit nearly identical green peaks and red absorption troughs. In M1 and M2, exactly 18 non-grassland patches were falsely classified as Grassland.

Case 2: Forest ↔ Transitional Woodland / Shrub

Ecological Overlap: Transitional vegetation represents an ecotone between open grassland and mature forest (young saplings, clear-cut regrowth, heathland). In visible bands, scrub mimics dense forest greenness (M1 F1=0.3529). In NIR, canopy micro-shadowing in mature forests attenuates reflectance relative to open multi-tiered scrub, enabling M2 to successfully resolve 36 of 42 shrub instances (M2 F1=0.9114).

6.3 Detailed Wetland Classification Failure Analysis

Across all three models at T=0.50, Wetland achieved an F1-score of exactly 0.0000 (0 TP, 0 FP, 27 FN out of 27 positive test patches). We investigated the underlying mechanisms:

  • Severe Training Imbalance (2.7% Prevalence): In tile T33UUP, the training split furnished only 8 positive Wetland patches out of 300 (292 negatives, 97.3%).
  • Loss Function Dynamics: Under unweighted Binary Cross-Entropy loss, misclassifying 8 positive patches contributes very little gradient signal compared to avoiding false alarms on 292 negative patches. Consequently, the network learned that outputting strongly negative logits for Wetland (z ∈ [-3.5, -0.8]) minimized overall loss.
  • Threshold Calibration Procedure: Candidate thresholds were swept over T ∈ [0.20, 0.80] strictly on validation set predictions. Because validation F1 remained 0.0000 across all candidate thresholds, the optimization algorithm retained T*=0.50 as the selected threshold. On the test set, predictions at T*=0.50 yielded 0 TP and 27 FN (F1 = 0.0000).

7. Conclusions and Limitations

7.1 Conclusions

  1. Multi-label recognition is essential: In our curated dataset, 94.4% of Sentinel-2 patches contain co-occurring land covers (mean: 3.00 classes per patch; test mean: 3.07).
  2. NIR (B08) provides the decisive performance boost: Adding NIR in M2 increased default-threshold Macro-F1 by +11.32 percentage points (0.5978 to 0.7110), boosted Exact Match from 12% to 38%, and resolved woody scrub confusion (Shrub F1 surging from 0.3529 to 0.9114).
  3. Interpretation of M3 Performance: M2 performed best on this curated test set at the default threshold of 0.5. M3 underperformed M2, achieving a Macro-F1 of 0.5673 compared with 0.7110 for M2. Small training-sample size, spectral collinearity, resampling effects, and optimization instability are possible explanations, but their individual contributions were not independently tested.
  4. Minority classes require dedicated loss weighting: Extreme training imbalance (8 Wetland patches) causes zero-recall failure under unweighted BCE loss.

7.2 Practical Limitations

  • Single-Tile Scope and Spatial Autocorrelation: All 500 patches originate from Tile T33UUP in the Austrian Alps. The splits guarantee patch-level disjointness but not geographic independence.
  • Intentional Class-Selection Bias: Capturing 100% of the tile's Wetland and Shrub patches enriched their prevalence (10.2% and 22.8%) relative to natural landscape baselines (1.6% and 3.6%).

7.3 Future Improvements

  • Positive-Class Weighting / Focal Loss: Apply positive class weights (wpos = Nneg / Npos) to penalize minority-class omissions.
  • Multispectral Transfer Learning: Pretrain the CNN backbone on continental-scale archives before fine-tuning on regional tiles.
  • Guided Super-Resolution: Downscale SWIR bands using 10 m visible band spatial guidance rather than unguided bilinear interpolation.
