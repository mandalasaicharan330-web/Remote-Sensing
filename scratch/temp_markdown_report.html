Abstract

Land-cover mapping is a foundational pillar of earth observation, environmental monitoring, agricultural assessment, and spatial planning. In heterogeneous natural and anthropogenic terrains, satellite image footprints frequently encompass multiple surface categories simultaneously (e.g., agricultural fields bordered by woodlots, or rivers traversing rural settlements). Conventional single-label classification assigns an exclusive category to each scene, fundamentally misrepresenting mixed landscapes. In this project, we implement and evaluate a multi-label remote sensing classification framework using Sentinel-2 multispectral imagery from the benchmark BigEarthNet v2.0 (reBEN) archive.

We curated and verified a 500-patch genuine dataset originating from Sentinel-2 tile T33UUP in the Austrian Central Alps, strictly partitioned into official, disjoint splits: 300 Training, 100 Validation, and 100 Test patches. We retained six strategically chosen diagnostic spectral bands: Blue (B02), Green (B03), Red (B04), Near-Infrared (B08), and Shortwave-Infrared (B11, B12). The 20\text{ m} SWIR bands were harmonized to 10\text{ m} (120 × 120 pixels) using bilinear interpolation, and all bands were radiometrically converted to physical Bottom-Of-Atmosphere (BOA) surface reflectance in [0.0, 1.0].

We trained a regularized 4-block Convolutional Neural Network (MultispectralCNN, ~617k parameters) under BCEWithLogitsLoss and conducted a controlled Band Ablation Experiment comparing:
- M1 (Visible RGB): Bands B02, B03, B04 (3 input channels)
- M2 (Visible + NIR): Bands B02, B03, B04, B08 (4 input channels)
- M3 (Full Multispectral): Bands B02, B03, B04, B08, B11, B12 (6 input channels)

At the standard decision threshold (T = 0.50), M2 achieved the highest overall performance, recording a test Macro-F1 of 0.7110, Micro-F1 of 0.8205, and Exact Match subset accuracy of 38.0%, significantly outperforming baseline M1 (Macro-F1 of 0.5978, Micro-F1 of 0.7403, Exact Match 12.0%). Adding NIR (B08) provided an immediate +11.32 percentage point gain in Macro-F1, critically resolving canopy-density confusion (Shrub/Transitional Vegetation F1 surged from 0.3529 to 0.9114) and sharply elevating Water F1 from 0.7429 to 0.8947 with 100% precision.

Conversely, M3 underperformed M2, recording a test Macro-F1 of 0.5673, Micro-F1 of 0.7081, and Exact Match ratio of 5.0% (T = 0.50). When decision thresholds were calibrated on the validation set, test Macro-F1 values were 0.7076 (M1), 0.7020 (M2), and 0.6559 (M3). On our 300-patch training split, adding two 20\text{ m} resampled SWIR channels expanded input dimensionality without sufficient sample support, leading to optimization instability and severe false-positive over-prediction for Grassland/Pasture (57 false positives; precision 40.0%). Furthermore, all three models recorded 0.0000 F1 on Wetland at T = 0.50 (0 true positives out of 27 test instances) because only 8 positive Wetland patches existed in the training split (2.7% prevalence). We discuss these findings in terms of remote sensing physics, small-sample dimensionality trade-offs, and multi-label threshold calibration.

1. Introduction

1.1 The Imperative for Satellite Land-Cover Mapping

Automated, high-fidelity land-use and land-cover (LULC) identification from spaceborne platforms is essential for quantifying environmental change, assessing crop phenology, modeling hydrological basins, and monitoring deforestation. Modern multispectral instruments, notably the European Space Agency's (ESA) Sentinel-2 constellation, provide global systematic coverage with 5-day revisit frequencies, offering 10 m and 20 m spatial resolutions across visible, near-infrared (NIR), and shortwave-infrared (SWIR) wavelengths.

1.2 Limitations of Single-Label Classification

In conventional remote sensing classification, each observation unit (pixel, polygon, or spatial tile) is forced into a single mutually exclusive category:

y ∈ \{1, 2, ..., K\}

However, at standard satellite patch footprints (1.2\text{ km} × 1.2\text{ km}, corresponding to 120 × 120 pixels at 10\text{ m} resolution), genuine landscapes are intrinsically heterogeneous mosaics. A single patch frequently contains cultivated arable fields bordering woodlots, alpine pastures framing stream channels, and rural built-up settlements. Single-label classification arbitrarily suppresses subordinate classes or fails on mixed pixels.

1.3 Multi-Label Formulation

To model landscape complexity without artificial mutual exclusivity, the ground truth for each patch is structured as a binary multi-hot vector:

y = [y_1, y_2, ..., y_K]^T ∈ \{0, 1\}^K

where y_k = 1 denotes the confirmed presence of land-cover category k, and y_k = 0 denotes its absence. Under this formulation, multiple surface types can legitimately evaluate to 1 simultaneously. In our curated dataset, 82.0% of patches contain ≥ 2 co-occurring classes (mean: 3.08 classes per patch).

1.4 Central Research Questions

  1. Can visible, NIR, and SWIR observations accurately identify multiple co-occurring surface types in complex landscapes?
  2. Does adding NIR (B08) and SWIR (B11, B12) consistently improve classification accuracy over standard visible RGB, and how does spectral dimensionality interact with small training sample sizes?

2. Dataset and Spectral Bands

2.1 The BigEarthNet v2.0 (reBEN) Dataset

We utilize the open-access BigEarthNet v2.0 benchmark archive (Clasen et al., 2024; Sumbul et al., 2019, 2021; bigearth.net), released by the Remote Sensing Image Analysis (RSiM) and DIMA groups at Technische Universität Berlin. BigEarthNet v2.0 represents a major scientific upgrade over v1.0:
- Atmospheric Correction: All Sentinel-2 tiles are processed to Level-2A Bottom-Of-Atmosphere (BOA) surface reflectance via sen2cor v2.11.
- Label Integrity: Ground truth labels were re-extracted using the CORINE Land Cover 2018 database (CLC2018 v2020_u1).
- Official Splits: Partitions are assigned across geographic tiles to prevent spatial autocorrelation leakage.

- Landscape Context: Alpine and sub-alpine topography (coniferous and mixed forests, managed pastures, agricultural valleys, settlements, mountain streams).

4. Reflectance Preprocessing & Harmonization

  1. Bilinear Spatial Resampling:
    Bands B11 and B12 have a native resolution of 20\text{ m} (60 × 60 pixels). We resample them to the 10\text{ m} working grid (120 × 120 pixels) using bilinear interpolation (PIL.Image.BILINEAR, order=1). Bilinear interpolation computes distance-weighted averages of four adjacent pixel centers, generating smooth, continuous spatial gradients without ringing artifacts (unlike bicubic) or artificial blocky steps (unlike nearest neighbor).
  2. Radiometric Scaling to Surface Reflectance:
    In accordance with the ESA Sentinel-2 L2A product specification, surface reflectance \rho is quantified as \text{DN} = 10,000 × \rho. We apply:

    \rho = \text{clip}\left(\frac{\text{DN}}{10,000.0}, 0.0, 1.0\right)

Division by 10,000 converts raw DN to Bottom-Of-Atmosphere surface reflectance. Clipping to [0.0, 1.0] handles rare specular glint highlights (e.g., solar reflections on glass roofs, greenhouse panels, or calm water surfaces > 10,000\text{ DN}, which represent < 0.0042\% of all pixels).

6. Multi-Label Deep Learning Methodology

Mathematical Formulation

Given an input multispectral cube X ∈ ℝ^{C × 120 × 120}, the neural network computes unconstrained raw logits z = [z_1, z_2, ..., z_K]^T ∈ ℝ^7.

Unlike multi-class classification which employs a softmax activation (\sum_k p_k = 1), multi-label classification treats each class as an independent Bernoulli trial. Independent class probabilities p̂_k ∈ [0, 1] are obtained via the standard logistic sigmoid function:

p̂_k = σ(z_k) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z_k}}

Loss Function

The model is optimized using Binary Cross-Entropy with Logits (BCEWithLogitsLoss), combining the sigmoid operation with binary cross-entropy:

LBCE(z, y) = -(1/K) ∑k=1..K [ y_k \cdot \log σ(z_k) + (1 - y_k) \cdot \log(1 - σ(z_k)) ]

Binary Decision Thresholding

Binary predictions ŷ_k ∈ {0, 1} are formed by applying a decision threshold vector \mathbf{T} = [T_1, ..., T_7]:

ŷ_k = 𝕀(p̂_k ≥ T_k)

We evaluate:
1. Standard Default Threshold (Tk = 0.50): Canonical baseline for unweighted cross-entropy.
2. *Validation-Calibrated Thresholds (Tk^): Selected by grid search over T ∈ [0.20, 0.80] on the validation split to maximize validation Macro-F1. Crucially, test labels are never accessed during calibration.

CNN Architecture (MultispectralCNN)

Implemented in src/model.py, the network extracts spatial-spectral features without excessive parameterization:
- Block 1 (120×120 → 60×60): \text{Conv2d}(C \to 32, 3 × 3) \to \text{BN} \to \text{ReLU} \to \text{Conv2d}(32 \to 32, 3 × 3) \to \text{BN} \to \text{ReLU} \to \text{MaxPool2d}(2, 2)
- Block 2 (60×60 → 30×30): \text{Conv2d}(32 \to 64, 3 × 3) \to \text{BN} \to \text{ReLU} \to \text{Conv2d}(64 \to 64, 3 × 3) \to \text{BN} \to \text{ReLU} \to \text{MaxPool2d}(2, 2)
- Block 3 (30×30 → 15×15): \text{Conv2d}(64 \to 128, 3 × 3) \to \text{BN} \to \text{ReLU} \to \text{Conv2d}(128 \to 128, 3 × 3) \to \text{BN} \to \text{ReLU} \to \text{MaxPool2d}(2, 2)
- Block 4 (15×15 → 7×7): \text{Conv2d}(128 \to 256, 3 × 3) \to \text{BN} \to \text{ReLU} \to \text{MaxPool2d}(2, 2)
- Global Pooling & Head: \text{AdaptiveAvgPool2d}((1, 1)) \to \text{Flatten} \to \text{Dropout}(0.4) \to \text{Linear}(256, 128) \to \text{ReLU} \to \text{Dropout}(0.3) \to \text{Linear}(128, 7)
- Trainable Parameters: M1: 616,679; M2: 616,967; M3: 617,543.

Training Configuration

  • Optimizer: AdamW (\text{lr} = 1.0 × 10^{-3}, \text{weight_decay} = 1.0 × 10^{-4})
  • LR Scheduler: CosineAnnealingLR (T_{\max} = 35, \eta_{\min} = 1.0 × 10^{-5})
  • Batch Size: 16 (19 batches/epoch)
  • Early Stopping: Monitored on validation loss (patience = 8 epochs)
  • Seed Policy: seed=42 across Python, NumPy, and PyTorch

8. Discussion of NIR and SWIR Results

8.1 The Physical Impact of NIR (B08): Spongy Mesophyll Scattering

Adding Band B08 (842\text{ nm}) provided an immediate +11.32 percentage point increase in default-threshold Macro-F1 (0.5978 to 0.7110) and more than tripled the Exact Match subset accuracy from 12.0% to 38.0%.
1. Shrub/Transitional Vegetation Breakthrough:
In visible RGB (M1), young saplings, clear-cut regrowth, and heath shrubs display chlorophyll absorption identical to closed mature forest, yielding an F1 of only 0.3529 (33 false negatives). In NIR, however, the multi-tiered architecture of open scrub scatters radiation differently from dense forest canopies with deep structural shadowing. Consequently, M2 recovered 36 of 42 shrub instances, elevating F1 to 0.9114 (+55.85 pp).
2. Hydrological Delineation (Water):
Liquid water strongly absorbs near-infrared radiation. Pure water bodies exhibit near-zero reflectance at 842\text{ nm}. Adding B08 allowed M2 to attain 100% precision on water (0 false alarms) and boosted recall from 61.9% to 80.95%, increasing F1 from 0.7429 to 0.8947 (+15.18 pp).

8.2 The Impact of SWIR (B11, B12): Small-Sample Dimensionality Trade-Off

In broad continental benchmarks (> 100,000 patches), SWIR typically aids classification. However, on our 300-patch training split, M3 underperformed M2 (Macro-F1 0.5673 vs 0.7110):
1. Curse of Dimensionality on Small Samples: Moving from 4 channels (M2) to 6 channels (M3) expanded the input space by 50% without additional training data, causing optimization instability.
2. Spatial Resampling Uncertainty: B11 and B12 originate at 20\text{ m} native resolution. Bilinear interpolation smooths pixel grids but cannot synthesize genuine 10\text{ m} spatial edges.
3. Severe Grassland Over-Prediction: M3 generated 57 False Positives on Grassland/Pasture (predicting pasture in 95 of 100 test patches), collapsing precision to 40.0% and F1 to 0.5672. Furthermore, water recall dropped to 28.57% (15 omissions).

Scientific Takeaway: This experiment does not establish that SWIR is universally unhelpful; rather, it demonstrates that without pretraining or large-scale regularization, increasing spectral dimensionality on small datasets can induce overfitting.

9. Error Analysis and Wetland Classification Failure

9.1 Diagnostic Breakdown of Test Predictions

At the standard T = 0.50 threshold:
- Exact Match Ratio: 38.0% in M2, 12.0% in M1, and 5.0% in M3. Because exact match requires all 7 binary decisions to match the ground truth simultaneously, higher label cardinality (average 3.08 labels per patch) makes this metric demanding.
- Dominant Confusion Pair: Agriculture versus Grassland/Pasture. In M1 and M2, exactly 18 patches were falsely classified as Grassland due to spectral similarities between tilled clover meadows and grazed alpine turf.

9.2 The Wetland Classification Failure

Across all three models at T = 0.50, Wetland achieved an F1-score of exactly 0.0000:
- Ground Truth Wetland patches in Test: 27
- True Positives (\text{TP}): 0
- False Positives (\text{FP}): 0
- False Negatives (\text{FN}): 27

Why Did the Models Fail on Wetland?

  1. Severe Training Imbalance (2.7% Prevalence): In tile T33UUP, the official training split provided only 8 positive Wetland patches out of 300 (292 negatives, 97.3%).
  2. Loss Formulation Dynamics: In unweighted Binary Cross-Entropy loss, misclassifying 8 positive patches contributes very little gradient signal compared to avoiding false alarms on the 292 negative patches. Consequently, the network learned that outputting strongly negative logits for Wetland (z_{\text{wetland}} ∈ [-3.5, -0.8]) minimized overall loss.
  3. Threshold Calibration Insight: When tuning decision thresholds on the validation set, lowering the threshold in M3 to T=0.20 yielded non-zero validation recall, but on the test set it introduced false alarms in damp agricultural fields.

10. Conclusions and Limitations

10.1 Conclusions

  1. Multi-label recognition is essential: Over 82% of Sentinel-2 patches in European landscapes contain co-occurring land covers.
  2. NIR (B08) provides the decisive performance boost: Adding NIR in M2 increased default-threshold Macro-F1 by +11.32 percentage points (0.5978 to 0.7110), boosted Exact Match from 12% to 38%, and resolved woody scrub confusion (Shrub F1 surging from 0.3529 to 0.9114).
  3. M2 performed best on this curated test set: M3 achieved 0.5673 Macro-F1, demonstrating that adding SWIR on small sample sizes without pretraining can degrade generalization through overfitting.
  4. Minority classes require dedicated loss weighting: Extreme training imbalance (8 Wetland patches) causes zero-recall failure under unweighted BCE loss.

10.2 Practical Limitations

  1. Single-Tile Geographic Scope: All 500 patches originate from Tile T33UUP in the Austrian Central Alps. The findings reflect Central European temperate alpine geography and cannot be claimed to generalize to Mediterranean, Scandinavian, or arid biomes.
  2. Intentional Class-Selection Bias: To enable evaluation of rare classes, we collected 100% of the tile's Wetland and Shrub patches. This enriched Wetland to 10.2% and Shrub to 22.8% across the 500 patches, diverging from raw landscape prevalence (1.6% and 3.6%).

10.3 Future Improvements

  • Positive-Class Weighting / Focal Loss: Apply positive class weights (w_{\text{pos}} = N_{\text{neg}} / N_{\text{pos}}) to penalize minority-class omissions.
  • Multispectral Transfer Learning: Pretrain the CNN backbone on continental-scale archives before fine-tuning on regional tiles.
  • Guided Super-Resolution: Downscale SWIR bands using 10 m visible band spatial guidance rather than unguided bilinear interpolation.
